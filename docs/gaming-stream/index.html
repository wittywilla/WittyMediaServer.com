Games: one stream for every device

How a game’s picture, sound and controls travel between the server and every client: browsers on computers and phones, the Apple app on Apple TV, iPhone, iPad and Vision Pro, and the Android app on phones, tablets and Android TV, at home and away. This is the decision, the numbers behind it, and the exact wire protocol, version 2. For everything else about playing a game (starting it, saves, controller layouts, settings), see the games API.

On this page
  1. The decision
  2. The protocol, version 2
  3. What each platform builds

The decision#

Every client uses WebRTC, through one route, with the same messages. A browser has WebRTC built in. The Apple and Android apps use Google’s libwebrtc, as prebuilt packages: LiveKit’s webrtc-xcframework (iOS, iPadOS, tvOS 17 and later, visionOS and macOS) and its Android package io.github.webrtc-sdk:android. Both are BSD-licensed builds of the same code a browser runs, with H.264 decoded in hardware through VideoToolbox and MediaCodec.

The one fallback is the same messages over a WebSocket, for a network that won’t carry UDP to the server: a reverse proxy, a rented server in front, or a guest Wi-Fi that blocks it. It’s a property of the network, not the platform, so every client has it. JPEG stays for a server with no H.264 encoder at all.

Version 1 (/play/{sid}/webrtc and /play/{sid}/ws) keeps working, unchanged, until every app has moved. Version 2 needs WittyPlay 0.3.5 or later; with an older one, the server’s modes don’t offer it, and its routes answer needs-update.

Why#

Measured on this Mac (M3 Pro, 12 cores, macOS 27.0.1) with wittyplay-probe, which plays a game through the real runner, encoder and transport into a real browser (Chrome 156, headless) and reads frame numbers back off the picture it shows. Every server part was a private copy on 127.0.0.1; nothing reached another machine. Each line is 20 to 30 seconds of play. The Mac was busy with other work during these runs (load average 7 to 80 on 12 cores), so 95th percentiles are noisier than an idle server’s; the medians agree run to run. The test share has no Nintendo DS game, so the DS wasn’t measured: its picture, 256x384, is about the SNES’s doubled, which the SNES lines cover.

At home, on a clean network, the transports are equal. Press to picture, in milliseconds, median and 95th percentile:

GameWebRTCWebSocket + H.264BitrateServer CPU, % of one core
NES (FCEUmm, Thwaite), 512x448 sent4.5 / 14.52.5 / 3.65.9 Mbit/s18 (encoder 9, runner 4, rest 5)
SNES (Mesen-S, SNES_00), 512x478 sent9.3 / 17.96.5 / 10.04.1 Mbit/s43 (runner 30, encoder 9)
NES, JPEGnot measured by the probe13 (no FFmpeg)

WebRTC’s figure includes the wait for the browser’s compositor, which the WebSocket’s canvas path skips; the browser’s own jitter buffer held frames for 0.5 ms (the playout-delay extension at work). Both are well under a frame.

Away, the transports part. The same NES game through the probe’s simulated networks, which add delay, jitter, loss and a speed limit to everything between the server and the browser (UDP for WebRTC; TCP for the WebSocket, where loss is modelled as TCP recovers from it: the lost segment comes a round trip late, or three after a burst’s tail, and everything behind it waits):

Press to picture, in milliseconds, median and 95th percentile. It includes the round trip itself.

Network: round trip, jitter, loss, speedWebRTCWebSocket, version 1WebSocket, version 2
Home Wi-Fi: 6 ms, 4 ms, 0.5%16 / 4214 / 3817 / 56
Away: 40 ms, 8 ms, 1%58 / 10653 / 17151 / 170
A bad connection away: 60 ms, 15 ms, 3%92 / 173235 / 419236 / 396
A slow upload at home: 40 ms, 3 Mbit/s63 / 1472,356 / 4,65058 / 231

Version 1’s WebSocket on a slow link is a queue that only grows: it sends 6 Mbit/s into a 3 Mbit/s link with nothing to slow it down, and at over 2 seconds behind (2.0 to 2.8 s in three runs) the game can’t be played. WebRTC comes down to fit within seconds. Version 2’s WebSocket now does too, from the client’s reports of how late frames arrive: its median matches WebRTC’s, and its 95th percentile (231 and 567 ms in two runs) is mostly the first seconds, before the rate has found the link. What it can’t fix is loss: its third line is TCP waiting for every lost packet, as version 1’s is, and there WebRTC is two and a half times quicker. A frame’s gap at the 95th percentile, which is what a stutter looks like, was 30 to 90 ms over WebRTC on these networks.

Over a WebSocket, a lost packet’s wait looks to the server like a queue, so on a lossy link the bitrate comes down too (four steps on the 1% and 3% lines). The picture is softer there; it isn’t later.

What decided it:

  • Loss. TCP hands data over only in order, so one lost packet holds up every frame behind it until it’s resent. WebRTC resends just that packet (NACK), and a frame that can’t be finished is skipped while the next keyframe is fetched. At 1% loss that’s the 95th percentile above; at 3%, the median too. Mobile networks and busy Wi-Fi lose that much in bursts.
  • Rate. WebRTC tells the server what’s arriving, every second, and the server matches the video to it (rateControl). Over a WebSocket the system’s buffers hide a full link from the server for a second or more, so version 2 has the client report how late frames arrive. That works (the last line above), but settles a few seconds later than WebRTC.
  • Sound. WebRTC carries Opus at about 100 kbit/s. The WebSocket carried raw PCM, 1 to 1.5 Mbit/s, which is a large share of an upload away from home. Version 2 sends Opus over the WebSocket as well.
  • Getting through. Over Tailscale or NetBird, the recommended remote access, WebRTC reaches the server directly, as at home. Through a reverse proxy or a forwarded HTTPS port only, it can’t, and the WebSocket can. So both stay, in that order.
  • Native apps. tvOS, iOS and Android have no WebRTC of their own, so the apps add libwebrtc: about 10 to 20 MB. That’s the whole cost, and it’s paid once; in return the apps get loss recovery, congestion control, Opus, NAT traversal and the same jitter-buffer behaviour as the browser, none of which they’d otherwise have to write.

What was weighed and set aside#

OptionVerdict
A tuned WebSocket for everyoneSimplest for the apps, and as fast as WebRTC at home. Away, TCP’s in-order delivery turns every loss into a stall for every frame behind it, as measured above. Kept as the fallback, tuned
WebTransport (QUIC) for browsers, raw QUIC in the appsEvery browser has WebTransport since Safari 26.4 (March 2026), and QUIC’s datagrams don’t stall on loss. But it brings none of what WebRTC gives for free: no jitter buffer, no resends, no congestion control tuned for video, no NAT traversal, and it needs a trusted certificate or a pinned one renewed every 14 days. Android has no QUIC of its own and Apple’s has no WebTransport, so the apps would each write a client from scratch. It doesn’t pass a reverse proxy either, so the WebSocket would still be needed. Worth a second look only if WebRTC’s jitter buffer ever becomes the problem
A UDP protocol of our own for the appsThe apps can open UDP sockets, but browsers can’t, so browsers would still need WebRTC: two protocols to keep in step, not one
HEVC or AV1Not for these games. A retro console’s picture, doubled, is 512 pixels wide, and H.264 carries it at 4 to 6 Mbit/s with every device decoding it in hardware. HEVC is arriving in browsers’ WebRTC (Chrome’s from about version 136) and most devices decode it in hardware; AV1 only recent ones. The video list in the hello lets a client say what it decodes, so HEVC can be offered later for the large pictures (Nintendo 64, PlayStation 2 and PSP, upscaled) on servers with a hardware HEVC encoder. Nothing here encodes it yet
Hardware encoding on this MacFFmpeg’s h264_videotoolbox holds frames back before it gives one out, so with WittyPlay’s one-frame-at-a-time feeding it stalls, is restarted, and falls back to libx264 (seen in every run). libx264 at 512x448 costs 8 to 10% of one core here, so a Mac server encodes these consoles in software. Larger pictures need the graphics card work in progress elsewhere

The protocol, version 2#

Finding it#

GET /games/modes and a session’s modes list what the server can stream, best first, as before. A mode reachable through version 2 says so with "stream": 2. A client that knows version 2 uses /play/{sid}/stream for those, and the version 1 routes for anything else.

{"id": "webrtc", "video": ["h264", "vp8"], "audio": "opus", "input": "datachannel", "stream": 2}

Try WebRTC first. If ICE hasn’t connected within 5 seconds, or no picture has come within 8, connect the WebSocket. The game waits, paused, for a minute with nobody connected, so trying costs nothing. Keep the old picture up until the new one shows.

The hello#

Everything a client says about itself, in one JSON object. It’s the same over both transports.

{

  "device": "appletv-livingroom",
  "name": "Living room",
  "watch": true,
  "video": ["h264"],
  "audio": ["opus", "pcm"],
  "seats": [0],
  "display": {"hz": 60, "width": 3840, "height": 2160},
  "latency": "auto",
  "maxKbps": 0
}
FieldMeaning

deviceThis device’s id, as used for touch layouts: up to 64 letters, digits, ., _ or -

WebRTC#

  1. Make a peer connection with no ICE servers: the server’s answer carries every candidate it has.
  2. If watching, add a receive-only video transceiver and a receive-only audio transceiver.
  3. Make the data channels: input (unordered, no retransmits, binary) and events (ordered, reliable, text). timing (unordered, no retransmits) is still accepted, but timing messages can now go on input as well.
  4. Make the offer, set it, and wait for ICE gathering, up to 1.5 seconds.
  5. POST /play/{sid}/stream with {"hello": {...}, "sdp": "<offer>"}. It answers {"stream": {...}, "sdp": "<answer>"}.
  6. Set the answer. The stream’s description is also sent as the first message on events once it opens.

The video is H.264 Constrained Baseline (profile-level-id=42e01f, packetization-mode=1), or VP8 when the offer has no H.264 or the hello asks for it first. Offer the http://www.webrtc.org/experiments/rtp-hdrext/playout-delay header extension: the server sets it to “don’t wait” (or “up to 80 ms” for balanced), so a frame reaches the decoder as soon as it’s whole. libwebrtc honours it on every platform. NACK and PLI work as usual: a lost packet is resent, and a decoder that’s lost track asks for a keyframe. Sound is Opus, 48 kHz stereo, 10 ms packets, in its own MediaStream so its buffer never holds the picture back.

WebSocket#

GET /play/{sid}/stream, upgraded to a WebSocket, with the sign-in as for any route: the Authorization header from an app, the cookie or ?token= from a browser. The first message must be the hello, as text, within 5 seconds:

{"type": "hello", "v": 2, "watch": true, "video": ["h264"], "audio": ["opus", "pcm"], "seats": [0]}

The server answers with the stream’s description, {"type": "stream", ...}, and then the picture, starting with a keyframe. A hello that’s missing, late or wrong is answered with {"type": "error", "code": "bad-hello", "text": "..."}, and the connection closes. Everything else is the same as over WebRTC: binary messages are what would go on input, and text is what would go on events. The server keeps at most two frames waiting for a slow connection. Past that it drops the frame, waits for a keyframe, and lowers the bitrate, so delay can’t build up. Sound and events are never dropped.

The stream#

Seats: several players on several devices#

A game has up to 8 players (ports). Each device can hold some of them:

  • Players are 0 to 7; the stream’s ports says how many ports this game has, and devices what each takes.
  • A hello with "seats": [1] takes player 2 for this device. To change, send {"type": "seats", "ports": [0, 1]}. Add "take": true to take a player another device has; otherwise that one is refused with an error. A device that connects again with the same device id gets its own players back.
  • Input for a player another device holds is ignored. A device that names no seats (as every version 1 client) can play any player nobody holds.
  • Whenever seats change, everyone gets {"type": "seats", "seats": [{"port", "conn", "name", "you"}]}.
  • When a device lets a player go, or leaves, that player’s buttons are released.
  • Rumble for a held player goes only to the device holding it. Rumble for a player nobody holds goes to everyone.

Every device of the account that started the game can join it this way, up to 8 at once, including a phone with "watch": false used only as a controller. Other accounts can’t join a game.

Input#

Binary messages, little-endian, on input or the WebSocket. The first byte says what each is.

Controller state: as in version 1, 13 bytes, or 11 without triggers, or 17 with a number of your own on the end:

BytesFieldMeaning
0portThe player, 0 to 7
1-2buttonsu16, a bit per RetroPad button, below
3-4, 5-6left stickX, Y: i16, -32768 to 32767, with up negative
7-8, 9-10right stickX, Y
11, 12L2, R2How far each trigger is held, 0 to 255. Set their button bits too, past your press point
13-16numberOptional u32 of your choosing. It comes back in frame records (0xF4), to follow a press to the screen
BitButtonBitButton
0B (bottom face)8A (right face)
1Y (left face)9X (top face)
2Select10L

5Down13R2

Send the whole state as soon as anything changes, and again every 250 ms. The channel doesn’t resend, so a lost state is put right by the next. Release everything when the app goes to the background or a menu opens. Which button of a real pad is which bit comes from the controller as data and the account’s profiles.

Touch screen: 6 bytes, for a console whose pad has "pointer": true:

BytesFieldMeaning
0mark0xFF
1flagsBit 0: pressed
2-3Xi16, -32767 at the picture’s left edge to 32767 at its right
4-5Yi16, -32767 at the top to 32767 at the bottom

Send it when anything changes, every 250 ms while pressed, and a release three times. It’s one finger; for several, use:

Touches, several fingers at once: 2 bytes, then 6 for each finger, up to 10.

BytesFieldMeaning
0mark0xFE
1countHow many fingers follow, 0 to 10
then, for each finger:
+0idYours to choose, so a finger keeps its place in the list while it moves
+1flagsBit 0: pressed

Send the whole list when anything changes, and every 250 ms while a finger is down. When the last one lifts, send an empty list (count 0) three times. The core sees the fingers that are pressed, in the list’s order: libretro’s pointer 0 is the first, and its count is how many. The touch screen is the first player’s, so either message needs player 0 (seats).

Motion: 14 bytes, for a game that reads a controller’s or a phone’s movement:

BytesFieldMeaning
0mark0xE1
1portThe player
2-3, 4-5, 6-7accelerometerX, Y, Z: i16 in 1/4096 g (±8 g), gravity included, the device held as the console’s controller: X right, Y up, Z towards the player
8-9, 10-11, 12-13gyroscopeX, Y, Z: i16 in 1/16 degree a second (±2048), turning about the same axes, anticlockwise positive

Send it for a player you hold while the game wants it: the stream’s motionOn, and then a {"type": "motion", "offered": true, "port": 0, "accel": true, "gyro": false, "hz": 60} event whenever that changes ("offered": true with no port says only that the core can read motion). Send it hz times a second (60 at most), and stop when neither accel nor gyro is on. The core reads it through libretro’s sensor interface, in metres a second squared and radians a second, as RetroArch gives cores a phone’s sensors. A core that never asks gets nothing. Servers on Windows or a 32-bit system don’t offer the sensor interface yet: there the stream’s motion stays false.

Rumble comes back as an event:

{"type": "rumble", "port": 0, "effect": 0, "motor": "strong", "strength": 40000, "ms": 300}
FieldMeaning
portThe player. Only the device holding it gets it; with nobody holding it, every device
motorstrong, the heavy, low motor (the left grip on most pads), or weak, the light, high one (the right). effect is the same as libretro numbers it: 0 strong, 1 weak
strength0 to 65535. 0 stops that motor at once
msHow long to keep that motor at that strength if nothing else comes. While it’s on, the server says it again every 200 ms, so it carries on smoothly; a connection that drops stops within ms

Play it on that player’s controller, or the device itself, unless the account’s profile says noRumble. A paused game’s motors stop, and start again when it carries on. libretro has no trigger motors, so neither does this.

Lights: {"type": "led", "index": 0, "state": 1} when the game sets one of its lights, and the stream’s leds lists them as they are. state is 0 for off, otherwise on, or a brightness up to 255 for a core that has one. They’re the console’s own lights (a drive light, a lamp on an arcade cabinet), through libretro’s LED interface. A client shows them where it has a light to spare. libretro has no colour light bar: light a controller’s player number, or its light bar’s colour, from seats.

Timing and control, optional, as in version 1:

First byteFromRest
0xF1ClientA ping: an f64 of your own clock
0xF2ServerThe answer: that f64, then an i64 of the server’s Unix nanoseconds
0xF3ClientOne byte: 1 to get a record of every frame, 0 to stop

The picture and sound over the WebSocket#

First byteRest
0x11Video: u8 flags (bit 0: keyframe), u32 frame number, u32 timestamp in 90 kHz, then one frame of H.264 in Annex B, with SPS and PPS before each keyframe
0x12Opus: u32 timestamp in 48 kHz, then one packet: 10 ms, 48 kHz, stereo
0x03PCM: u32 sample rate, then signed 16-bit stereo. For a client that left out opus
0x02One JPEG, when video is jpeg

Both timestamps count from the same moment, so a client that wants to can line sound up with the picture. Show each frame as soon as it’s decoded; don’t hold frames to their timestamps.

Frame pacing#

A console doesn’t run at 60 frames a second exactly: the NES and Super Nintendo run at 60.0988, the DS at 59.83, PAL consoles at 50.007. On a 60 Hz screen, a 60.0988 game drops a frame every ten seconds, which shows as a hitch in anything that scrolls.

  • The server runs the game at the screen’s rate when they’re within 1% of each other. It takes display.hz from the device holding player 1 (or the first that’s watching), and the sound is resampled to match, so nothing is audibly different: 60.0988 to 60 is 0.16% slower. pace in the stream says when it’s doing this.
  • Apps show each frame at the next refresh, and line frames up with it using 0xF6: when frames land just after a refresh, ask for them earlier. PhaseLock in WittyPlay’s web/latency.ts is the browser’s version. An app knows its refresh exactly (CADisplayLink, Choreographer), which makes this simpler than in a browser.
  • A TV app switches the display to the game’s rate when the TV has it: 50 Hz for a PAL game, through AVDisplayManager.preferredDisplayCriteria on tvOS and Surface.setFrameRate on Android TV. Then say the new rate with a report (below), and the server matches it.

Sound and picture together#

Sound and picture are separate streams, so that sound’s buffer never delays the picture. Sound is behind by whatever the device holds back to play smoothly: 35 to 80 ms in a browser. Apps can do better: hold 20 to 40 ms and follow audio.latency from the person’s settings.

Stats#

{"type": "stats", "on": true} on events (or as text on the WebSocket) asks for stats once a second; "on": false stops.

{
  "type": "stats", "gameFps": 60.1, "sentFps": 60.1, "encodedFps": 60.1, "encodeMs": 0.7, "kbps": 5800,
  "width": 512, "height": 448, "encoder": "libx264", "speed": 1, "paused": false, "pace": 60,
  "conn": {"transport": "webrtc", "video": "h264", "audio": "opus", "kbps": 5800, "share": 1, "rttMs": 2, "lost": 0, "queueMs": 0, "dropped": 0, "seats": [0]}
}

share is how much of the full bitrate is being sent after matching the network. rttMs and lost come from WebRTC’s reports; queueMs and dropped are what a WebSocket connection is waiting on and has lost to waiting.

A client can report what it sees: {"type": "report", "hz": 60, "delayMs": 12, "rttMs": 24, "jitterMs": 3, "decodeMs": 1.1, "displayMs": 6, "dropped": 0}. Every field is optional. hz is the screen’s refresh rate, for pacing; send it when it changes. delayMs matters over a WebSocket: how much later than the quickest lately the frames are arriving, from each 0x11 frame’s timestamp against when it arrived (the web player’s ArrivalDelay). Send the worst of each half second, twice a second. The server lowers the bitrate when it keeps growing, as it does for WebRTC’s round trips. The rest is for the server’s log.

Errors#

POST /play/{sid}/stream and the WebSocket’s upgrade answer as any route does, with a code beside error:

StatuscodeMeaning
400bad-helloThe hello is missing or wrong. error says which field
401, 403Sign in again, as for any route
404no-sessionNo such game for this account, or it has ended
413too-largeThe offer is over 64 KB
415no-codecNothing in common: no H.264 or VP8 in the offer, or none this server can encode
424no-moduleWittyPlay isn’t installed on this server
429busyToo many connections at once. Wait for Retry-After
500failedSomething went wrong starting it. Try again, then fall back
501needs-updateThis server’s WittyPlay is older than version 2 of the stream (before 0.3.5). Its modes don’t say "stream": 2 then, so a client that follows them never sees this. Use version 1’s routes

While connected, refusals come as {"type": "error", "code": "...", "text": "..."}: seat-taken (with port), bad-seat and bad-message. The game ending comes as {"type": "ended", "code": "...", "text": "..."}, and the connection then closes:

codeMeaning
stoppedIt was saved and stopped: by DELETE /play/{sid}, or because the account started another game somewhere
quitThe game shut itself down
crashedThe emulator stopped or stopped answering. What was saved stays
idleNobody pressed anything for 30 minutes
evictedToo many devices connected: this, the oldest, went

text is written for a person and can be shown as it is.

Rate#

The server starts at a bitrate for the picture’s size, at half that for a device it counts as away from home, and never above the lowest maxKbps of the devices watching. It then follows the network: from WebRTC’s receiver reports (loss and round trips), and over a WebSocket from the client’s delayMs reports, how long frames wait to be written, and frames dropped. It comes down quickly and goes back up slowly. One encoder serves every device watching, so the device with the worst network sets the rate for all; a game is usually watched on one screen, and a controller-only device costs nothing.

What each platform builds#

Browsers (WittyPlay’s player): done. It sends a hello through /stream on both transports, holds seats for the players its controllers are on, decodes Opus over the WebSocket with WebCodecs, reports the screen’s refresh rate, and keeps rumble to its ms. Not yet: several fingers, motion and lights, which browsers give pages little of.

Apple (Apple TV, iPhone, iPad, Vision Pro):

  1. Add LiveKit’s webrtc-xcframework (Swift Package, tvOS 17 or later). Take decoded frames as CVPixelBuffers through an RTCVideoRenderer and draw them with the existing Metal path, so display.* effects still apply.
  2. Connect as above: data channels input and events, the hello with video: ["h264"], the seats of the controllers connected, and display.hz from the screen.
  3. Keep the current ws-h264 code as the fallback, moved to /stream with a hello, and decode Opus with AVAudioConverter (kAudioFormatOpus), falling back to PCM by leaving opus out. Send delayMs reports twice a second from the 0x11 timestamps, or a slow network fills with video.
  4. Rumble from the rumble event through Core Haptics: motor strong on the controller’s .leftHandle locality and weak on .rightHandle (or both on .default for one motor), each for ms unless renewed.
  5. Motion from GCController.motion (gravity plus user acceleration in g, rotation rate in radians a second), or Core Motion on an iPhone or iPad held as a pad, while motionOn lists a player this device holds.
  6. Several fingers with 0xFE on iPhone and iPad touch play; the Siri Remote stays one pointer.
  7. led events and seats on the controller’s lights: GCController.playerIndex, and GCDeviceLight for a DualSense’s light bar.
  8. On tvOS, switch the display to the game’s fps with AVDisplayCriteria where the TV has it, and send a report with the new hz.

Android (phones, tablets, Android TV):

  1. Add io.github.webrtc-sdk:android. Render frames through a VideoSink into the player’s own surface, so effects still apply; libwebrtc decodes H.264 with MediaCodec.
  2. Connect as above, with the same hello. Phones with "watch": false can be controllers for a game on a TV.
  3. The WebSocket fallback through /stream, with Opus decoded by MediaCodec (audio/opus), or PCM, and delayMs reports twice a second from the 0x11 timestamps.
  4. Rumble through the controller’s VibratorManager: with two vibrators, strong on the first and weak on the second, each for ms unless renewed; the phone’s own for touch play.
  5. Motion from the controller’s own sensors (InputDevice.getSensorManager(), Android 12 and later) or the phone’s SensorManager when it’s the pad, converting m/s² and rad/s to the wire’s units, while motionOn lists a player this device holds.
  6. Several fingers with 0xFE for touch play.
  7. led events and seats on the controller’s lights (InputDevice.getLightsManager(), Android 12 and later).
  8. On Android TV, call Surface.setFrameRate(fps, FRAME_RATE_COMPATIBILITY_FIXED_SOURCE) and report the new hz.

Every app: use /play/{sid}/stream when a mode says "stream": 2, and the version 1 routes otherwise. The rest of the game API is unchanged.

Where it lives#

  • WittyPlay: stream/connect.go (the hello, the stream and seats), stream/viewer.go (both transports), stream/wsv2.go (the WebSocket), stream/feedback.go (rate), stream/haptics.go (rumble, lights, motion), libretro/libretro.go and libretro/sensor_*.go (the sensor and LED interfaces, several fingers), host/ (pacing, and carrying them to the core), web/transport.ts, web/arrival.ts and web/refresh.ts (the browser’s side), cmd/wittyplay-probe (measuring, with -v2, and -impair on TCP as well as UDP).
  • WittyMediaServer: internal/server/api_games.go (the two routes, and which devices count as away), internal/play/stream (the module’s interface, generated).
